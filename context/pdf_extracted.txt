Abstract
SatQuery AI is an agentic Earth-observation intelligence platform that lets analysts interrogate
satellite imagery in natural language. Instead of forcing users to choose sensors, bands, models,
and GIS operations by hand, the system inspects the uploaded imagery, classifies the user’s intent,
plans an execution graph, dispatches specialist remote-sensing models (detection, segmentation,
spectral indices, change detection, optical–SAR fusion), grounds every claim in spatial evidence,
and returns an auditable answer with confidence and a full execution trace. This document
surveys the relevant literature (remote-sensing captioning, visual question answering, visual
grounding, bi-temporal change detection, change description, optical–SAR fusion, and geospatial
foundation models), catalogues the forty features proposed in the project’s concept notes,
critically de-duplicates and prioritises them into MVP / SIH-demo / advanced / research tiers,
specifies the end-to-end analysis pipeline and software architecture, and closes with an evaluation
framework, a design-decision log, a professional risk register, a dependency-aware build order,
and a team learning roadmap. The central engineering thesis is that a vision–language model
alone is not the product: the defensible contribution is the orchestration layer that routes natural
language to verified specialist computation and binds every answer to evidence.
How to read this document
Every non-trivial claim in this document carries one of five epistemic labels, following the
critical-analysis requirement of the design brief:
• [Fact] — supported by peer-reviewed literature or official documentation (cited).
• [Proposal] — our engineering design for SatQuery AI; not yet validated.
• [Assumption] — plausible but must be validated during the build.
• [Experimental] — requires a prototype and measurement before adoption.
• [Future] — deliberately outside SIH scope.
Proposed features are never presented as existing capabilities.

Project Overview
1.1
What SatQuery AI is
SatQuery AI is an interactive vision–language assistant for multimodal remote-sensing image
analysis through text queries. Its intelligence layer, AERIS (Agentic Earth Reasoning & Intelligence
System), converts a natural-language request over one or more satellite images into a planned,
executed, and audited geospatial analysis. The concept notes describe it as an agentic Earth-
observation intelligence platform rather than a satellite chatbot: the differentiator is that answers
are produced by verifiable specialist computation (spectral indices, segmentation, detection, change
detection, optical–SAR fusion) and only explained by a language model — never invented by one.
Elevator pitch
You give SatQuery AI satellite imagery and ask questions in plain language — “where did new
buildings appear?”, “show me unhealthy vegetation”, “what changed between the optical and
the SAR image?” The system figures out what kind of data it is looking at, picks the right
remote-sensing tools, runs them, highlights the exact regions that support its answer, and tells
you how confident it is and how it got there. It turns satellite-image analysis from a specialist
GIS workflow into a conversation — without hiding the evidence.
Technical definition [Proposal]. SatQuery AI is a deterministic-routing, tool-using multimodal
analysis system: a query-understanding front end (classifier + planner) maps natural language and
uploaded rasters to a typed execution graph over a registry of specialist models and geospatial
operators; an evidence engine binds intermediate products (masks, boxes, index maps, change maps)
to claims; and a vision–language model (VLM) performs perception-level tasks (VQA, captioning,
grounding) and synthesises explanations strictly from structured, validated results.
1.2
The SIH problem statement
The project targets Smart India Hackathon 2026 problem statement SIH26167 (ISRO): an
interactive vision–language assistant for multimodal remote-sensing image analysis through text
queries. The statement’s core demands map onto four system obligations: (i) accept multimodal
Earth-observation input (at minimum optical and SAR); (ii) accept text queries as the primary
interaction mode; (iii) perform analysis, not just description — detection, localisation, comparison,
quantification; and (iv) remain interactive — follow-up questions, region-scoped queries, and
iterative investigation.
1.3
The core problem being solved
Operationally, remote-sensing analysis today is a toolchain problem. Answering even a simple
question such as “did built-up area increase here?” requires an analyst to know which sensor is ap-
propriate, acquire and preprocess the data (atmospheric correction, cloud masking, co-registration),
choose and run a change-detection method, post-process the result, quantify area, and interpret it.
Each step has its own software, parameters, and failure modes. The bottleneck is not the availability
of algorithms — it is the selection, sequencing, and interpretation of algorithms. SatQuery AI
attacks exactly that bottleneck.

1.4
Why conventional remote-sensing analysis is difficult
• Data heterogeneity [Fact].
Optical multispectral, SAR, thermal, and hyperspectral
products differ in geometry, radiometry, noise model, and semantics; a SAR backscatter image
cannot be treated as an RGB picture (Section 3).
• Preprocessing burden [Fact]. Georeferencing, CRS handling, resampling, co-registration,
cloud masking, and atmospheric correction are prerequisites for almost any analysis and are
a common source of silent error.
• Scale [Fact]. A single Sentinel-2 tile is roughly 10,980 × 10,980 pixels per band at 10 m;
naive full-scene inference is infeasible, so tiling and spatial reconstruction are mandatory.
• Expertise gap. The people with the questions (disaster responders, planners, agricultural
officers) are usually not the people who can operate the toolchain.
1.5
Why multimodal satellite imagery is difficult to analyse
Optical and SAR are complementary but non-aligned observations of the same ground truth. Optical
sensors measure reflected solar radiation in spectral bands; SAR measures coherent microwave
backscatter sensitive to structure, moisture, and geometry, works at night and through clouds,
but suffers speckle, layover, foreshortening, and shadow [Fact; Section 3]. Fusing them requires
radiometric and geometric normalisation, co-registration to sub-pixel accuracy, and models that
respect that optical̸ = SAR at the pixel-interpretation level.
1.6
Why natural-language interaction is useful
Natural language is the shortest path between an analyst’s question and the system’s capabilities. It
removes the requirement to know which tool implements which analysis, allows iterative refinement
(“now only the north-east part”), and produces a self-documenting session: the query log is the
analysis narrative. It does not remove the need for rigour — which is why the language layer must
sit on top of verifiable computation, not replace it.
1.7
Why a VLM alone is insufficient
Critical analysis — the core argument
A generic or even remote-sensing-adapted VLM cannot be the whole system, for five reasons:
1. Hallucination without evidence [Fact]. Generative models produce fluent answers
unsupported by the pixels; without grounding, there is no way to audit a claim.
2. No quantitative rigour [Fact]. “How many hectares changed?” requires georeferenced
pixel counting, not token prediction. VLMs are poor at precise counting, measurement,
and spatial arithmetic.
3. Band blindness [Fact]. Standard VLMs consume 3-channel RGB. NDVI, NDWI,
SAR polarimetry, and 13-band Sentinel-2 products are invisible to them unless specialist
preprocessing renders or computes them.
4. Weak temporal/cross-modal reasoning [Fact]. Bi-temporal change reasoning and
optical–SAR comparison are active research problems (Sections 7, 8, 9); off-the-shelf
VLMs do not solve them reliably.
5. No provenance [Fact]. A chat answer carries no trace of inputs, parameters, model
versions, or intermediate products — unacceptable for scientific or operational use.
Therefore: the VLM is a component, not the architecture. Specialist models produce
structured, checkable results; the VLM perceives and explains.

1.8
Why specialist remote-sensing tools/models are required
Indices (NDVI, NDWI, NDBI, NBR) are closed-form band arithmetic with decades of validation;
segmentation and detection models (U-Net, SegFormer, task-specific detectors) produce pixel-
accurate, measurable outputs; change-detection networks (Siamese/transformer/Mamba families)
are purpose-built for bi-temporal comparison. These are deterministic or statistically evaluated
tools with known error characteristics — exactly what an evidence-first system needs as its ground
truth layer.
1.9
What makes this different from a satellite-image chatbot
• Evidence-first answers: every claim links to a region, mask, model, and execution trace
(Section 20).
• Tool routing, not monolithic generation: a planner dispatches specialist models (Sec-
tion 12).
• Multimodal and temporal by design: optical, SAR, and bi-temporal inputs are first-class,
with compatibility validation.
• Quantified output: areas, percentages, counts — computed geospatially.
• Auditable: provenance, confidence, and reproducibility are system features, not afterthoughts
(Section 21).
1.10
Intended end-to-end user experience
Upload imagery →ask a question →watch the system state its plan →receive an answer with
highlighted evidence, confidence, and a trace →drill in (“investigate”, “ask this region”, “show me”)
→export a report. The concept notes’ scripted dialogue (“built-up area increased by approximately
18.4%, 14.2 hectares, primarily north-east, confidence 91%”) is the target interaction quality.
1.11
Novelty and technical contribution
Novelty is claimed only at the integration level [Proposal]: combining natural-language routing
+ specialist remote-sensing tools + evidence grounding + temporal and cross-modal reasoning +
auditable traces in one SIH-feasible system. Each individual ingredient exists in the literature
(Section 28); the combination, and the engineering discipline around evidence, is the contribution.
We explicitly do not claim a new foundation model or a new algorithm.
1.12
What should realistically be demonstrated in an SIH prototype
SIH demo target (reasoned in Section 25)
A judge should be able to: upload an optical GeoTIFF and a SAR image, ask single-image
questions (VQA, captioning, grounding, indices), run a bi-temporal change query with a
quantified, localised answer, see cross-modal evidence, inspect the execution trace, and export
a report — live, in minutes, on a single GPU. Continuous monitoring, voice control, 3D Earth,
and autonomous multi-step investigation are explicitly not required for a credible demo.

3.1
Remote-sensing fundamentals
3.1.1
Electromagnetic spectrum and sensing modes
Remote sensing measures electromagnetic (EM) radiation interacting with the Earth’s surface.
Passive sensors record reflected sunlight or emitted thermal radiation (optical, multispectral,
hyperspectral, thermal); active sensors emit their own signal and record the return (SAR, LiDAR)
10
SatQuery AI / AERIS — Technical Design & Study Document
SIH 2026 — SIH26167
[Fact]. Key spectral regions for this project: visible (∼0.4–0.7 µm), near-infrared (NIR, ∼0.7–
1.3 µm), short-wave infrared (SWIR, ∼1.3–3 µm), thermal infrared (∼8–14 µm), and microwave (e.g.
C-band, ∼5.6 cm for Sentinel-1).
3.1.2
The four resolutions
• Spatial resolution — ground sampling distance (GSD): the ground size of one pixel (Sentinel-
2: 10/20/60 m; Landsat 8/9: 30 m multispectral; MODIS: 250–1000 m). Determines the
smallest detectable object.
• Spectral resolution — number and width of bands. Multispectral: few broad bands
(Sentinel-2: 13). Hyperspectral: hundreds of narrow contiguous bands.
• Temporal resolution — revisit time (Sentinel-2: ∼5 days with two satellites; Landsat: 16
days; MODIS: daily). Governs change-analysis cadence.
• Radiometric resolution — quantisation depth / sensitivity to intensity differences (Sentinel-
2: 12-bit). Affects index precision and subtle-change detection.
3.1.3
Multispectral, hyperspectral, optical, thermal, SAR
Optical/multispectral imagery records reflected solar radiation in discrete bands — intuitive, rich in
spectral semantics (vegetation, water, soil), but blocked by clouds and darkness. Hyperspectral adds
fine spectral discrimination (minerals, species, materials) at high data and processing cost. Thermal
records emitted radiation (surface temperature, urban heat, fires). SAR (synthetic aperture radar)
actively illuminates the surface with microwaves and records backscatter amplitude and phase;
Sentinel-1 provides C-band VV/VH polarisations. SAR penetrates clouds and works at night
and is sensitive to structure, roughness, and dielectric properties (moisture), but exhibits speckle
(multiplicative granular noise), and geometric distortions: layover (tops of structures mapped
toward the sensor), foreshortening (slopes compressed), and radar shadow (no-return zones) [Fact].
Consequence for SatQuery AI: SAR outputs must never be interpreted with optical intuition; the
explanation layer needs modality-aware phrasing [Proposal].
3.2
Important satellite data concepts
3.2.1
Missions relevant to SatQuery AI
• Sentinel-1 (ESA/Copernicus) — C-band SAR, VV/VH (IW mode), ∼10 m pixel spacing,
6–12 day revisit; the project’s primary SAR source.
• Sentinel-2 (ESA/Copernicus) — 13-band multispectral, 10–60 m, ∼5-day revisit; primary
optical source; L2A products are atmospherically corrected (Sen2Cor) and include a scene
classification (cloud) layer.
• Landsat 8/9 (USGS/NASA) — 30 m multispectral + 15 m panchromatic + thermal;
16-day revisit; 50+ year archive — the reference for long-term change.
• MODIS (NASA) — 250–1000 m, daily; coarse but temporally dense.
• VIIRS (NOAA/NASA) — 375–750 m, daily; night-lights band relevant to urbanisation.
• ISRO datasets — Cartosat series (high-resolution panchromatic/multispectral, e.g. Cartosat-
3 ∼0.25 m pan), RISAT series (C-band SAR), Resourcesat (LISS-III/IV), EOS-04 (RISAT-1A).
Accessible via the Bhuvan geoportal and MOSDAC; licensing and bulk-access terms must be
verified before training use [Assumption].
3.2.2
Data artefacts the system must understand
• Bands and metadata: band order/wavelengths, acquisition time, sensor mode, processing
level, nodata value, scale/offset.

• Georeferencing and CRS: the affine geotransform mapping pixel (col, row) to projected

continuous data).
• Co-registration: aligning two scenes to sub-pixel agreement; mandatory before any bi-
temporal or cross-modal comparison (Section 7).

fills [Proposal; see Section 13.4].

Previous Research: Remote-Sensing Image Captioning
4.1
Evolution of the task
Remote-sensing image captioning (RSIC) has passed through five technical generations [Fact]:

1. CNN–RNN encoder–decoder (mid-2010s): a CNN encodes the image; an LSTM decodes
a sentence, following the “show and tell” paradigm. Early RS adaptations produced short,
generic descriptions (“many buildings are near a road”).
2. Attention-based captioning: soft attention lets the decoder focus on image regions while
generating words, improving object mention and reducing generic output.
3. Transformer-based systems: self-attention encoders/decoders [8] replaced recurrence,
enabling longer-range context and better fluent, structured descriptions.
4. Multimodal pretraining: CLIP-style contrastive image–text alignment [11] provided
transferable features; captioning became fine-tuning on top of aligned encoders.
5. Large vision–language models: instruction-tuned LVLMs (LLaVA-style [13]) adapted
to RS — RSGPT [24], RS-LLaVA [26], GeoChat [25], SkyEyeGPT [27], LHRS-Bot [28],
EarthGPT [29] — unify captioning with VQA and grounding in one model.
4.2
From generic description to geospatial semantics
Progress has moved descriptions along five axes: (i) land-cover semantics (correct class vocabulary);
(ii) geographic semantics (place-aware phrasing); (iii) object relationships (“harbour with docked
ships”); (iv) spatial descriptions (“in the north-east corner”); and (v) domain terminology (“built-
up”, “cropland”, “sediment plume”). Benchmarks such as UCM-Captions, Sydney-Captions, RSICD,
NWPU-Captions, and the human-annotated RSICap [24] drove this progression; VRSBench [23]
now evaluates captioning jointly with VQA and grounding.
4.3
Limitations of generic VLMs on satellite imagery
Why not just use GPT-4V-class or LLaVA-class models directly?
• Top-down domain shift [Fact]: nadir views, tiny objects, unusual textures, and absence
of canonical object scales degrade encoders trained on ground-level photos.
• Resolution mismatch [Fact]: RS scenes are megapixels; VLM vision encoders expect
∼224–448 px inputs — naive downsampling destroys exactly the detail the question
targets.
• Weak spatial reasoning [Fact]: VLMs are unreliable at counting, relative position,
and area estimation in overhead imagery; benchmarks repeatedly show large gaps versus
specialised models.
• No spectral access [Fact]: RGB-only input discards NIR/SWIR/SAR information
that defines the semantics of RS scenes.
Domain-adapted RS-VLMs close part of this gap; SatQuery’s routing + specialist tools close
the rest [Proposal].

Task formulation

aerial imagery. VRSBench [23] broadened evaluation to captioning, grounding, and VQA with
human-verified annotations.

embeddings (the original RSVQA baselines); (ii) domain-adapted VLMs — instruction-tuned

outputs (masks, boxes, change regions) to language in the evidence layer (Section 20) [Proposal].

Bi-Temporal Change Detection
7.1
Task and prerequisites
Bi-temporal change detection takes co-registered images IT0, IT1 and produces a change map —
binary (changed/unchanged) or semantic (from-class →to-class). Two prerequisites dominate all
downstream quality [Fact]:
• Image registration: geometric alignment via georeferencing + (if needed) feature-based
refinement (affine/homography); misregistration of even 1–2 pixels creates systematic false
change along edges.
• Radiometric normalisation: season, illumination, and atmosphere differences mimic
change; use same-season pairs, surface reflectance, and cloud masking.
7.2
Optical–optical change detection
Canonical use cases: Sentinel-2→Sentinel-2 seasonal comparison, construction monitoring, vegeta-
tion loss, water expansion, disaster damage. The architectural evolution [Fact]:
1. CNN differencing / early fusion: concatenate or difference bi-temporal features; CVA-
style baselines.
2. Siamese CNNs: weight-shared encoders with late fusion — FC-EF, FC-Siam-conc, FC-
Siam-diff [35]; SNUNet adds dense skip connections [40]; DSIFN adds deep supervision
[41].
3. U-Net decoders: Siamese encoders + U-Net-style decoders became the default dense-
prediction recipe [19].
4. Transformers: BIT tokenises bi-temporal features and models context with a transformer
[38]; ChangeFormer hierarchical transformer backbones [39]; STANet introduced spatial–
temporal attention with the LEVIR-CD dataset [37].
5. Hybrid and state-space models: CNN–transformer hybrids; Mamba/state-space ap-
proaches (ChangeMamba’s MambaBCD/SCD/BDA [42], building on Mamba [18]) target
global context at linear cost.
Benchmarks: LEVIR-CD [37], WHU-CD [43], OSCD [36], S2Looking [44], SYSU-CD [45], and the
semantic-change dataset SECOND [46].
7.3
Optical–SAR change detection
Optical–SAR pairs (Sentinel-1 + Sentinel-2) combine spectral with structural information and
enable cloud-resistant monitoring. Difficulties [Fact]: (i) heterogeneous appearance — the same
object looks unrelated across modalities, so pixel-level differencing is meaningless; comparison
must happen in feature/semantic space; (ii) cross-modal alignment — radiometric and geometric
normalisation plus co-registration; (iii) fusion strategy — per-modality feature extraction followed
by feature-level or decision-level change reasoning. Multimodal change datasets remain scarce
relative to optical–optical, which is why SatQuery AI treats optical–SAR change as comparative
evidence (per-sensor analysis + joint interpretation) rather than a single learned cross-modal change
network for the SIH build [Proposal; see Section 9].

The concept notes list change detection, segmentation, classification, description, and VQA

only trustworthy when they verbalise validated masks and statistics. SatQuery AI therefore
implements change description/VQA as structured-results-to-language rendering over the change
pipeline’s outputs, optionally cross-checked by a change-VQA model — not as free-form VLM
generation [Proposal]. This directly implements the evidence-first principle and matches the
direction of CDQAG-style research [49].

Optical–SAR Multimodal Fusion
9.1
Fusion strategies
• Early (data-level) fusion: stack co-registered channels (e.g. S-2 bands + VV/VH) and
train one encoder. Simple; sensitive to misregistration and radiometric mismatch; supported
by paired datasets such as SEN12MS [52] and BigEarthNet-MM [53].
• Feature-level (intermediate) fusion: modality-specific encoders; merge features via
concatenation, addition, or gating. The default for heterogeneous sensors.
• Late (decision-level) fusion: independent per-modality predictions combined by rules or a
meta-model. Most robust to missing/degraded modalities — and the most auditable, since
each modality’s evidence stays separable.
• Cross-attention fusion: queries from one modality attend to the other’s features; used in
recent multimodal transformers and fusion-oriented foundation models (CROMA [59], DOFA
[60], SkySense [61]).
• Semantic-prior-guided / frequency-aware fusion / mixture-of-experts: research
directions that route or weight modality contributions by learned priors, frequency content,

or expert gating [Experimental for SatQuery AI].
9.2
When to fuse and when not to
Fusion policy for SatQuery AI (Proposal)
Fuse at feature or decision level, never raw-pixel level. Use fusion when tasks benefit
from complementarity: built-up/water mapping under clouds, flood mapping (SAR water +
optical context), structural confirmation of optical change. Keep modalities separate when:
co-registration quality is poor; the question is purely spectral (NDVI) or purely structural (SAR
texture); or auditability demands per-sensor evidence. SatQuery AI’s default is late fusion
with joint explanation: optical evidence + SAR evidence →fused conclusion, with each side
inspectable — mirroring the concept notes’ Cross-Modal Analysis Lab.

12
Agentic / Task-Routing Approaches for Remote Sensing
12.1
Why not send every query to an LLM
Routing every query to a single LLM/VLM fails on correctness (Section 1.7), cost (per-token pricing
for quantitative work that band arithmetic does for free), latency (GPU-bound generation vs.
millisecond index computation), and auditability (no intermediate artefacts). The correct pattern
is language in, computation out: language models parse intent and render explanations; specialist
tools do the measuring.
12.2
The routing pipeline
SatQuery AI routing chain (Proposal)
User Query →Intent Classification →Modality Detection →Data Validation →Specialist Tool
Selection →Analysis →Evidence Extraction →VLM/LLM Explanation →Visualization →Audit
Trace
• Query classification / intent detection: map the query to a typed intent (SCENE_VQA,
CAPTION, GROUND, INDEX_QUERY, DETECT, SEGMENT, CHANGE_DETECT, CHANGE_VQA, CROSS_MODAL,
REPORT, MONITOR). A lightweight classifier (fine-tuned small model or LLM with structured
output) suffices; intents are few and well-separated [Proposal].
• Modality detection and validation: from metadata — bands, resolution, CRS, polar-
isation, acquisition date; validate compatibility (bi-temporal needs 2 co-registrable scenes;
NDVI needs NIR+Red; SAR questions need SAR input). Fail fast with an explanation, never
silently degrade.
• Specialist model selection: consult the model registry (name, version, capabilities, input
modalities, supported tasks, latency, inference function) and pick the cheapest tool that
satisfies the intent.
• Execution planning: for multi-step intents, emit a linear or DAG plan (e.g. registration →
change detection →building segmentation →intersection →area).
• Result validation and confidence: sanity checks (mask coverage, index ranges, registration
residual), plus model-reported confidence; low-confidence results are flagged, not hidden.
• Provenance: every step appends to the execution trace (Section 21).
12.3
Deterministic router vs. autonomous agent
Critical analysis — is an “agent” actually required?
The concept notes describe a fully agentic workflow (planner, model router, autonomous
investigation). For an SIH system, the safe and demonstrable core is a deterministic router
with constrained planning: a finite intent set, typed tool schemas, and template plans
per intent. Fully autonomous multi-step agents add failure modes (plan drift, tool-call loops,
unbounded cost) without adding demo value for the core query families. Recommendation:
implement the LangGraph-style state machine [81] with fixed routing edges per intent and

a bounded “investigate” macro (a pre-defined expansion sequence), reserving open-ended
autonomy for post-SIH work [Proposal; Future for full autonomy]. This preserves the concept’s
spirit — the user sees planning, tool selection, and traces — while keeping behaviour predictable
under judging.

Remote-Sensing Analysis Pipeline
15.1
The canonical 20-stage pipeline
S1. Data ingestion — upload or reference (STAC/Bhuvan [Future]); store raw file immutably.
S2. File identification — format sniffing (GeoTIFF/JP2/PNG), band count, driver.
S3. Metadata extraction — sensor hints, acquisition date, processing level, nodata, scale/offset.
S4. CRS detection — EPSG lookup; reject or repair missing CRS (with user confirmation).
S5. Band identification — map band indices to wavelengths (Sentinel-2 templates; user hint
fallback).
S6. Quality checking — nodata fraction, histogram sanity, resolution report.
S7. Cloud / no-data handling — SCL/s2cloudless masking for optical; flag coverage.
S8. Spatial normalisation — reproject to analysis CRS; align grids.
S9. Co-registration — for pairs: geometric alignment + residual estimate; abort/warn above
threshold.
S10. Resampling — harmonise band resolutions (e.g. SWIR 20 m →10 m grid).
S11. Tiling — windowed reads (e.g. 512 × 512, 10% overlap).
S12. Feature extraction — indices, embeddings, per-tile model features.
S13. Specialist analysis — detection / segmentation / change / SAR tools.
S14. VLM reasoning — VQA/captioning/grounding over rendered tiles or regions.
S15. Evidence localisation — bind outputs to georeferenced regions (masks, boxes).
S16. Answer generation — structured results →constrained LLM rendering.
S17. Visualisation — overlays, change maps, charts, side-by-side views.
S18. Confidence estimation — per-tool scores + validation checks →aggregate badge.
S19. Provenance logging — append full trace (inputs, versions, parameters, timings).
S20. Final response — answer + evidence + confidence + trace + export handles.

18.1
Overview
Architecture in one paragraph (Proposal)
A Next.js frontend talks to a FastAPI backend over REST (uploads, CRUD) and WebSockets
(streaming analysis progress). The backend hosts the LangGraph router, which dispatches to
specialist services (raster/index engine, CV models, VLM serving) running as Python workers
34
SatQuery AI / AERIS — Technical Design & Study Document
SIH 2026 — SIH26167
with GPU access. Rasters live in object storage; structured state (assets, missions, evidence,
traces) lives in PostgreSQL/PostGIS. Every run writes a provenance record that the frontend
renders as the execution trace and evidence views.
18.2
Frontend
Next.js + React + TypeScript + Tailwind [Proposal]. Map: MapLibre GL (2D raster overlays,
boxes, masks, change maps) as default; Leaflet acceptable; CesiumJS only for the ADV 3D Earth.
Charts for index/area statistics. Key inclusion rationale: MapLibre handles georeferenced overlays
natively; Cesium’s 3D adds no analytical capability — hence ADV, not MVP.
18.3
Backend
FastAPI (async Python) — chosen because the entire geospatial/ML stack is Python; one language
end-to-end removes an integration layer. REST for uploads/CRUD; WebSockets for streaming
execution-trace events and progress (judges see the plan unfold live — high demo value). Background
jobs via a simple task queue; heavy inference on GPU workers.
18.4
AI layer
Strictly separated concerns: VLM (perception + explanation rendering), LLM-class classifier
(intent), specialist CV models (detection, segmentation, change), embeddings (RemoteCLIP-
class for retrieval/zero-shot support), tool execution (deterministic Python tools: indices, area
stats, registration). No RAG document store in MVP — the knowledge the system needs is in the
imagery and tool outputs, not in documents [Proposal].
18.5
Geospatial layer
Rasterio (Pythonic raster I/O — primary), GDAL (underneath Rasterio; CLI for conversions),
GeoPandas + Shapely (vectors, intersections, area), pyproj (CRS), xarray (multi-band/time
stacks, optional), PostGIS (spatial queries over AOIs/detections), STAC (deferred to FUT for
catalogue ingestion). TorchGeo [80] recommended for dataset loaders and samplers.
18.6
Storage
PostgreSQL/PostGIS (assets, AOIs, detections, missions, evidence, traces) + object storage or
filesystem bucket (raw rasters, products, report PDFs) + in-memory/Redis cache for rendered tiles
(optional). No vector database in MVP (no semantic-search requirement).
18.7
Model serving
Hugging Face Transformers for VLM + PEFT/LoRA weights; ONNX Runtime as CPU fallback
for specialist models; vLLM/TorchServe unnecessary at SIH scale. Quantisation (8-bit/4-bit via
QLoRA-style loading) to fit a 7B-class VLM on a single ≤16 GB GPU; specialist models are small
enough to co-reside. CPU fallback exists for everything except acceptable-latency VLM chat —
which is why VLM calls are isolated behind M13 with graceful degradation [Proposal].
18.8
Explicitly excluded (with reasons)
Node.js backend (splits the language stack); Kubernetes (SIH overkill — Docker Compose suffices);
full microservice mesh (modules are Python packages behind one API first; split only if GPU
contention demands); vector database (no RAG); STAC ingestion (FUT); 3D Earth (ADV); voice
(FUT).

20
Evidence-Grounded Answers
Design principle: the LLM explains evidence; it never invents it
The answer pipeline is strictly: specialist model →structured result →spatial/temporal
evidence →validation →LLM explanation. The generation step receives only validated
structured results (masks, statistics, confidence) and is instructed to verbalise exactly them.
Every answer object contains: answer text; confidence; highlighted region(s) (georeferenced);
relevant evidence artefacts (index map, change mask, boxes); model/tool used with version; input
imagery identifiers; acquisition timestamps; and the processing trace. Example (concept-note target):
“Built-up area increased by approximately 18.4%; 14.2 hectares of new built-up regions, primarily
north-east; confidence 91%” — where 18.4% and 14.2 ha are computed from the georeferenced mask,
and 91% aggregates model confidence and validation checks.
Why this reduces hallucination and increases trust [Fact + Proposal]: generation
conditioned on structured, validated inputs has a drastically smaller fabrication surface than
free-form answering; every numeric claim is traceable to a computation; every spatial claim to pixels.
This is the same principle behind grounded-VQA research [49] and retrieval-grounded generation
generally. It also enables the “low confidence” UX: when evidence is insufficient, the system says so
and offers alternatives (“use another sensor”), which is more valuable than a fluent guess.

Evaluation Framework
SatQuery AI is evaluated per layer, not only on final chat answers — matching the concept notes’
multimodal-evaluation requirement.
22.1
Remote-sensing metrics
Segmentation/change: Intersection-over-Union IoU = |P∩G|
|P∪G|, mean IoU, Dice = 2|P∩G|
|P|+|G|, precision,
recall, F1 = 2PR
P+R, pixel accuracy; change detection reports F1/IoU on the change class (LEVIR-CD
convention). Detection: mAP at IoU thresholds (DOTA/DIOR convention).
22.2
VLM / VQA metrics
VQA: accuracy / exact match on RSVQA, VRSBench, CDVQA splits. Captioning/description:
CIDEr, BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR as proxies plus human evaluation on a fixed 50-scene panel
(fluency, correctness, terminology). Semantic-similarity scoring for free-form answers.
22.3
Grounding metrics
Box IoU@0.5 accuracy (REC convention), pointing accuracy (point-in-region), mask IoU for RES
outputs; evaluated on RSVG/DIOR-RSVG and VRSBench grounding splits.
22.4
System-level metrics
Routing accuracy (intent classification on a hand-labelled query set — target ≥95% on the six core
intents); tool-selection accuracy; end-to-end latency per query family; GPU memory; throughput;
failure rate (crashes, validation aborts); fallback activation rate.
22.5
User-level metrics
Task completion on scripted analyst tasks; analyst time saved vs. manual workflow; interpretability
rating (can the user find the evidence for a claim in <30 s?); usability (SUS-style questionnaire).
Why NLP metrics alone are insufficient
BLEU/ROUGE/CIDEr reward phrasing overlap, not correctness. A caption can score well
while mislocating every object; a change description can be fluent and numerically wrong.
SatQuery AI answers carry quantitative and spatial commitments, so evaluation must include
geospatial metrics (IoU, area error), grounding accuracy, and routing correctness — NLP scores
are reported but never sufficient [Fact + Proposal].

Feasibility Analysis
25.1
Can all 40 features be built?
No — not within an SIH cycle with a student team and one GPU, and attempting it would produce
forty shallow features instead of a credible system. The tiering below optimises for technical
credibility + working integration + strong demonstration, not feature count.
25.2
MVP — the smallest system that clearly solves the problem
GeoTIFF ingestion + metadata (#15,16), tiling (#17), geospatial engine (#18), index engine
(added), 2D map + exports (#19,39), RS-VLM VQA/captioning (#3–5), query router + registry +
traces (#1,25,26,29), land-cover from segmentation (#9 via pretrained model). Proof: upload
an optical scene, ask “what is here / where is the water / which regions have high NDVI”, get
evidence-linked answers with a visible trace.
25.3
SIH demo — the strongest realistic set
MVP + grounding (#6), detection and segmentation overlays (#7,8), bi-temporal change detection
with quantified, localised answers (#10), change description (#11), SAR ingestion + per-sensor
analysis (#13), evidence graph + confidence (#27,28), ask-this-region (#22,23), report generation
+ persistence (#38,40). Demo script: the concept notes’ built-up-change dialogue, live, ending in
a PDF report with embedded trace ID.
25.4
Advanced version — if time/resources permit
Change VQA model (#12), optical–SAR fusion view (#14), timeline scrubber (#21), autonomous
investigation macro (#24), model observability page (#30), missions and histories (#33,36,37), 3D
Earth (#20).
25.5
Research / future — do not attempt during the hackathon
Continuous monitoring + alerts (#34,35) — needs automated data acquisition, scheduling, and
storage budgets; voice (#2,32) — ASR adds a failure layer with no analytical value; bench-
mark/research mode (#31) — valuable only after the system exists; end-to-end text-to-mask RES
and learned cross-modal change networks — open research problems; multi-temporal (>2) reasoning
and hyperspectral support.

Reasoning summary
The cut lines follow three rules: (i) no feature without its dependency chain — monitoring
needs missions, storage, scheduling, and acquisition automation that SIH cannot supply; (ii) no
unverifiable claims — features whose output cannot be checked live (free-form change VQA
without grounding) are subordinated to checkable ones; (iii) demo value per engineering day
— 3D Earth and voice are high-cost, low-substance for judges evaluating an ISRO problem
statement.

Build Order
Dependency-aware sequence (Figure 1 gives the feature-level view).
Phase 0 — Fundamentals. Remote sensing + Python + Rasterio/GDAL basics; team reads
Section 3; hello-world: load S-2 GeoTIFF, print metadata, render RGB.
Phase 1 — Raster engine. Ingestion, metadata, CRS, tiling, index engine, basic viewer overlays.
Gate: NDVI map of a Sentinel-2 tile rendered in the UI.
Phase 2 — Specialist models. Segmentation (pretrained →fine-tuned), detection, change de-
tection (LEVIR-CD-trained), registration stage. Gate: change mask + area stats computed for
a test pair.
Phase 3 — VLM. RS-VLM serving (quantised), VQA + captioning over tiles. Gate: single-image
Q&A in chat.
Phase 4 — Query router. Intent classifier + deterministic routing over the six core intents.
Gate: routing accuracy ≥95% on labelled query set.
Phase 5 — Tool calling. Router dispatches specialist tools; structured results flow back. Gate:
“show unhealthy vegetation” runs index engine end-to-end.
Phase 6 — Evidence layer. Grounding links, evidence store, confidence badges, execution-trace
UI. Gate: every claim in a session is clickable to pixels.
Phase 7 — Full UI. Map + chat + evidence explorer + investigation workspace.
Phase 8 — Integration. Bi-temporal and cross-modal query paths end-to-end; report generation.
Gate: full demo script passes three consecutive runs.
Phase 9 — Evaluation. Benchmark runs (VRSBench subset, LEVIR-CD, CDVQA subset, rout-
ing set); latency/memory profiling; fix worst offenders.
Phase 10 — Demo hardening. Offline bundle, pre-computed fallback scenes, backup video,
rehearsed script, freeze.

Research Gaps and SatQuery AI’s Potential Novelty
28.1
What existing systems do not combine effectively
The survey shows clear islands of capability [Fact]: RS-VLMs converse but do not quantify;
change detectors quantify but do not converse; grounding models localise but not over validated
specialist outputs; fusion models combine sensors but not under natural-language task routing;
monitoring platforms exist but without conversational, evidence-linked interaction. No surveyed
system combines: natural-language interaction + multimodal RS + specialist analysis
tools + task routing + grounding + temporal reasoning + optical–SAR reasoning +
evidence-backed explanations in one auditable workflow.
